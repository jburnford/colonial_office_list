Justice is administered by courts at Zanzibar and Mombasa, and by inferior courts where administrative officers or magistrates preside. In all cases to which natives are parties local ideas and customs are considered. On the ten-mile coast strip domestic slavery is legal, but is practically extinct. No one born after August 1st, 1890, can be a slave, and the purchase of slaves is prohibited. In the rest of the Protectorate slavery is non-existent. The military force consists of 23 officers and 1,000 men, forming the 3rd Battalion of the King’s African Rifles; the 1st Battalion is also in the Protectorate pro tem. There are 1,800 police, under 33 European officers and non-commissioned officers.

Products and Trade.

The agricultural products of the lowlands are cocoa-nuts, rice, maize, and various native grains; cotton and tobacco are also being cultivated. Experiments with a view to a large extension of cotton growing are being made, and the introduction of other seeds and plants of economic value is being tried. In the highlands potatoes grow freely, and are now exported; coffee, wheat and barley are successfully cultivated, but not yet on a large scale. On the uplands enormous pasture grounds are available for cattle.

The forest products of the coast are rubber, gum-copal and timber. Mangroves grow on the coast, acacia and ebony in the scrub forests further inland. The highland forests yield rubber, fibres and castor-oil beans, and contain croton, olive, fig and several good timber trees. The greater part of the forest area is quite unexplored, though a large syndicate has now obtained a concession for cutting timber, and is surveying the forest north of the railway. Several large areas of fibre-bearing land have been leased near Voi and Kibwezi for the purpose of developing the Sanseviera fibre, which is indigenous.

The mineral resources of the Protectorate are not yet well ascertained, but iron is known to occur abundantly in most districts; mica and graphite are found in Ukamba; limestone is worked near Kitui, Makindu and Lake Victoria; opals are common in the Rift Valley; a large deposit of carbonate of soda has been found in the south part of Ukamba; gold mining began, but was discontinued. Recent concessions include prospecting rights and grants of agricultural and grazing land.

Foreign imports by sea are liable to duty at the rate of 10 per cent. ad valorem, but certain articles imported for agricultural, stock-breeding, railway and some other purposes are free. There are specific export duties.

In 1905-06 the chief imports were cotton goods, 210,571l.; rice, grain and flour, 67,090l.; spirituous liquors, 23,934l.; building material, 44,046l. The chief exports were ivory, 20,542l. from the Protectorate, 34,601l. from Uganda; copra, 9,431l.; grain, 27,097l.; rubber, 18,929l.; hides and skins, 16,320l. from the Protectorate, 40,300l. from Uganda, 46,250l. from German East Africa.

Of the imports the value of 227,951l. (34 per cent.) came from the United Kingdom; 173,875l. (26 per cent.) from India; 48,314l. (7·2 per cent.) from Germany. Of the exports the value of 38,796l. (31·5 per cent.) to the United States.

Mombasa is visited by the steamers of four lines. The vessels of the British India Steam Navigation Company, those of the Austrian Lloyd Company from Trieste, and those of the Messageries Maritimes call monthly; those of the German East Africa line, and those of a branch of this line from Bombay, call fortnightly. Communication between the ports of the Protectorate is maintained by a steamer belonging to the Administration.
