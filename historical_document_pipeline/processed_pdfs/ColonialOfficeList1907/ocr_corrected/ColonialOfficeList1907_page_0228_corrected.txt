CAPE OF GOOD HOPE.

Surveys have been made through the fertile and generally densely populated native districts of the Transkei and East Griqualand, with a view to future railway connection with the sister colony of Natal.

Parliament authorised by Act 38 of 1902 the construction of the following lines, which will further open up the eastern districts of the Colony:—

A 3 feet 6 inch gauge line from a point at or near Amabele loop to Butterworth, towards Umtata, a distance of 78 miles, at a cost of 519,045l.

A 3 feet 6 inch gauge line from Indwe to Maclear, towards Kokstad, for a distance of 107 miles, at a cost of 596,901l.

A 2 feet gauge line from Aliwal North to Gairtney (47 miles), towards Barkly East, at a cost of 148,849l.

The Amabele-Butterworth line has been opened for traffic as far as Eagle (54 miles), and is expected to be completed and opened for traffic to Butterworth in December, 1906. By Act 34 of 1906 Parliament has sanctioned the extension of this line from Butterworth to Idutywa (26 miles) in the direction of Umtata, at a cost of 117,842l.

The line to Maclear was opened for traffic on the 29th August, 1906. The total length of the branch from Sterkstroom to Maclear is 171 miles.

With regard to the Aliwal North-Gairtney line, the section from Aliwal North to Lady Grey has been constructed under Act 29 of 1904 as a 3 feet 6 inch gauge line, and is open for traffic. The construction, on a 3 feet 6 inch gauge, of the remaining portion to Gairtney (21 miles) has been authorised by Act 34 of 1906 at a cost of 197,515l.

Parliament, per Act 48 of 1905, authorised the construction by a company styled “The St. Johns-Kokstad Railway Company, Limited,” of a line of railway of the standard gauge from a point adjacent to the Umzimvubu River at the port of St. Johns to Kokstad. This line, which is 142 miles in length, is subsidised by the Government, the subsidy authorised being calculated at the rate of 1,500l. per mile.

Parliament, per Act No. 49 of 1905, also authorised the construction by a company styled “The St. Johns-Umtata Railway Company, Limited,” of a line of railway 76 miles in length from a point upon the proposed St. Johns-Kokstad Railway (authorised by Act 48 of 1905) at Isenuka to Umtata. The line authorised is of the standard gauge, and a subsidy will be paid by the Government in respect thereof, calculated at the rate of 1,500l. per mile. These two lines, when completed, will open up the native districts of Pondoland and the Transkei. The construction of these two lines has not yet been started.

The construction of the following two lines, to branch from the eastern system, has been authorised by Act 34 of 1906, viz.:—

A 2 feet gauge line from Cathcart to a point in the vicinity of Fairford or Chilton (49 miles), at a cost of 138,229l.

A 2 feet gauge line, Queenstown to Whittlesea (22½ miles), at a cost of 55,000l.

By the same Act the Cape Government is empowered to enter into a convention with the Natal Government for the construction and working, by the latter, at their own cost, of a line of railway from the Natal border to Riverside. In this connection Parliament has also authorised the construction, by the Cape, of a line from Riverside to Llewellyn (27 miles), at a cost of 256,000l.

On the 31st December, 1905, the mileage of the Cape Government Railways open for traffic was 2,987, and the length authorised and under construction on that date was 268 miles. The gross receipts for the twelve months ended December 31st, 1905, were 4,047,065l. compared with 4,144,319l. for the year 1904; the working expenses were 3,076,920l. compared with 3,719,822l. for the year 1904, resulting in a return of 3l. 6s. 10d. per cent. on capital expended as compared with 11l. 11s. 8d. per cent. for the previous year.

Through railway communication now exists between all the Government lines of the Cape Colony to Bulawayo, Victoria Falls and Broken Hill and Salisbury in Rhodesia; to Beira in Portuguese East Africa; to Bloemfontein, Johannesburg, Pretoria and Pietersburg on the Central South African Railways; and to Durban and other important centres in Natal, and Delagoa Bay in the Portuguese territory.

The existing colonial lines, in common with all South African lines (save the private line, 100 miles in length, owned and worked by the Copper Mining Company in Namaqualand, isolated from any Government line, which has a gauge of 2 feet 6 inches, and the 2 feet gauge lines referred to herein), are constructed on a gauge of 3 feet 6 inches. The lines are well and substantially laid, and both the rate of speed and their general comfort and equipment are admittedly creditable. Especial attention is devoted to the peculiar requirements of long journeys and the variations of climate experienced. Corridor saloons, carefully constructed with a view to comfort and stability, are provided on the through trains. They are fitted with lavatory accommodation and electric light, while the provision of bedding at reasonable charges adds materially to the comfort of the passengers.

Owing to the opening of the Klerksdorp-Fourteen Streams line, there are now (September, 1906) alternative routes to the Transvaal from Cape Town. There is a morning train daily from Cape Town to Johannesburg and Pretoria via Kimberley and Klerksdorp. On Tuesdays this train starts from the Cape Town docks for the convenience of passengers who arrive by the mail steamer from England, and conveys first, second and third class passengers direct from the docks at Cape Town to Kimberley and stations in the Transvaal, including Johannesburg, Pretoria, Klerksdorp and Potchefstroom; the train also serves stations in the Western district of the Cape Colony—Worcester, Matjesfontein, Laingsburg, Beaufort West, etc. A through sleeping saloon for Bulawayo and Victoria Falls is attached to the train and goes forward from Kimberley to Victoria Falls by the train de luxe, leaving the former station each Wednesday evening.

The dining and sleeping saloon train starts from the boat side, and completes the journey to Kimberley in 30½ hours, and to Johannesburg in 47 hours.

Passengers by mail steamers for up-country who are desirous of reaching their destinations as early as possible, and who do not wish to spend any time in Cape Town, are thus enabled to purchase their tickets, entrain at ship’s side, and continue their journey at once.

A railway official boards the vessels before disembarkment for the purpose of affording information to intending passengers, and special arrangements have been made with the Customs Department for dealing expeditiously with their baggage.

A conductor and also an experienced chef accompany the train, and passengers can obtain all meals on board at a fixed tariff as well as other refreshments at moderate charges.
