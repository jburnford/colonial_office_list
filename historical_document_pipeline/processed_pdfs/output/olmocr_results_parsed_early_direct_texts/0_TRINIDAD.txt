TRINIDAD

Is an island lying to the eastward of the continent of Venezuela, between N. latitude 10° 3', and 10° 50', W. longitude 61° and 62° 4' W. of Greenwich. Its length is about 60 miles, its breadth from 40 to 44 miles. It is separated from the continent of Venezuela, by the Gulf of Paria, into which falls the northern mouths of the Orinoco. In figure it is oblong. It was first discovered by Christopher Columbus, on the 31st July, 1498; and first colonized in 1588, by the Spaniards. In 1676 the French possessed it, but it was soon restored to Spain.

On the 12th February, 1797, an expedition for the reduction of Trinidad, sailed from Martinique, on the 14th it put into Carriacou, and sailed on the following morning with some additional transports. The naval command of this Expedition was entrusted to Rear-Admiral Henry Harvey. The troops, numbering 6750 men, were, commanded by Sir Ralph Abercrombie.

To this armament Don. J. M. Chacon had to oppose four fine line of battle ships, manned by 1,600 seamen and marines. The land force was was less than 500 men.

The expedition resulted in the surrender of the island to His Majesty's arms, and on the 18th Febr-
ruary, 1797, the articles of capitulation were signed by Abercrombie, Harvey, and Chacon.

Don José María Chacon left the island a few days after the capitulation. He and Admiral Apodaca were, on their arrival in Spain, placed under close arrest, and on the 28th May, 1797, were tried by a Court Martial, called in Spain, a Council of War, and were honourably acquitted.

Abercrombie, after making the best arrangements that the confused state of the Colony allowed, departed two months after, leaving his Aide-de-Camp, Lieutenant-Colonel Thomas Picton, as Governor, whose first act was to institute a Council of Advice, consisting of five members.

On the 29th March, 1802, the definitive treaty of peace between England and France, and her allies, viz.: Spain and the Batavian Republic, was signed at Amiens. By the third article, all places taken during the war by Great Britain, were to be restored, save Trinidad and Ceylon; and in the fourth article, "His Catholic Majesty cedes and guarantees, in full property and sovereignty, the Island of Trinidad to His Britannic Majesty."

The area of the island is 1,754 square miles.

Port of Spain is the chief town and port of entry. This town according to the Census of 1861, contains 18,980 inhabitants, of which 8,544 are males, and 10,436 females.

The second town and port of entry is San Fernando, 26 miles south from Port of Spain, with a population of 4,429 inhabitants. There are also the minor towns of St. John, St. Joseph, and Arima, all to the eastward of port of Spain.

The harbour is considered the finest in the West Indies.

The principal articles of produce exported, are sugar, rum, molasses, cacao, coffee, and cotton. The number of sugar estates do not exceed 152, and those of cacao and coffee 700. The total extent of land under cultivation is 68,592 acres, as follows: canes, 36,739 acres; cacao, coffee, and cotton, 14,238; provisions, 9,914 acres; pasture, 6,306 acres.

The population of the island, in 1861, amounted to 84,438: 46,074 males, and 38,364 females, of the following classification:—Trinidad, 46,936; British Colonies, 11,716; United Kingdom, 1,040; Foreign 4,301; China, 461; India, 13,488; Africa, 6,035; nondescribed, 461.

By the Ordinance, No. 16 of 1841, the island was for ecclesiastical purposes in regard to the Church of England, divided into 16 parishes.

In 1849, by the Ordinance No. 8, the island was divided into divisions, counties, districts, and wards. Subsequently the Ordinance No. 14, of 1854, was passed for the purpose of regulating the appointment of wardens, the raising of local rates for local purposes, and dividing the wards into ward unions.

Under the same last-mentioned Ordinance, there is also established a Central Road Board for the general management of all matters connected with the several wards, ward unions, and road unions, with a secretary who receives a salary of 500l. per annum.

The total annual revenue of the several ward unions, for roads, bridges, schools, police, hospitals, and other local purposes, amounts to 26,894l., sterling, and the amount expended by the several wards during 1863, in respect to education, amounted to 3,163l. 15s. ld. sterling.

The towns of Port of Spain, and San Fernando, are under the jurisdiction of Borough Councils, the former consisting of fifteen elective members, the latter of seven. One of the members is annually chosen mayor.

There is an Island Post Office, amalgamated with the General Post Office. There are nine established mail routes, besides four foot routes; the total transportation of mails on these routes being 65,058 miles, at a cost of 3,454l. sterling per annum, or 1s. 0½d. per mile.

There are 21 Post Offices throughout the island, the total delivery of letters averaging annually 111,682 packet letters, and 77,673 inland ones. There are two local steam-boats for the conveyance of mails, police, and prisoners, the contract cost of which is 4,500l. per annum. The total cost of this department is 8,500l.

Several Institutions are kept up at the public expense, viz. two general hospitals, a lunatic asylum, a leper asylum, a wash-house, and a public bath house.

The soil of the island is rich, and produces, in addition to the cane, cacao, coffee, and cotton, numerous kinds of timber, and the choicest of West Indian fruits.

A pitch lake, 99 acres in extent, is situated in the ward of La Brea, and is about 30 miles from port of Spain, it is of considerable value, and affords a slight revenue to the island. The largest portion of the lake belongs to the Earl of Dunonald, by lease from the Government.

Coal is also to be found in the ward of Manzanilla; it was tried on board of H. M. steamer "Buzzard," and was found to be of excellent quality.

Immigration from India and China, is fully encouraged by the Government. The amount voted in aid of that object in 1863, was 32,890l. By the census of 1861, there were 13,488 Coolies, and 461 Chinese. Since then there have been imported, in 1862, 1,967 Coolies, and 452 Chinese; and in 1863, 1,798 Coolies; in 1864 and 1865, 3,152 Coolies, and 591 Chinese; giving a total of 20,405 Coolies and 1,504 Chinese.

These immigrants (Cooles and Chinese) are located on 154 different estates. The largest number on any one estate is 206, the "Orange Grove Estate," county of St. George. The revenue collected on account of the annual outlay was, in 1863, 16,137l. sterling.

The places of worship in Port of Spain, are Trinity Church, and All Saints' Chapel, a Roman Catholic Cathedral, and three Roman Catholic Chapels, a Wesleyan Chapel, a Presbyterian, a Baptist, and a Portuguese Chapel.

The military barracks, "St. James's," are buildings, which do credit to Trinidad, spacious, and with every requisite accommodation. They were commenced on the 21st January, 1824, and completed on the 11th June, 1827, at the expense of 80,000l. sterling.

The Police Force of the island consists of an inspector, two sub-inspectors, one clerk, eight sergeants, sixteen corporals, and one hundred and thirty-four constables; the total annual cost of the establishment is 17,045l. The Prison Department consists of one inspector, one superintendent, one medical officer, a chaplain, a teacher, one overseer, two clerks, twenty-four turnkeys, and two matrons; the total annual cost of the establishment, including maintenance and keep of prisoners, and all other expenses, is 6,800l.

Constitution.

The Government is administered by a Governor and an Executive Council of 3 members. There is also a Legislative Council, including the Governor, who is President, 6 official and 8 unofficial members, all of whom are nominated by the Crown. There is no Representative Assembly.

TURKS AND CAICOS ISLANDS.

These islands were formerly included among the Bahama group, from which they were separated in 1848 by Act of the Bahama Legislature. The following is an extract from that Act:—“The Islands of Grand and Salt Cay, together with the small islands and Cays immediately adjacent thereto, and which, together with the said Islands of Grand and Salt Cay, are commonly known and designated as ‘The Turks’ Islands,’ and the Islands and Cays, commonly known and designated as the ‘Caicos Islands,’ together with all the banks and Cays, situate, lying, and being to the eastward of the said Turks’ Islands and Caicos Islands, and comprised within, or deemed part of, or appertaining to, Your Majesty’s Bahama Islands, shall cease to be component parts of the Bahama Government,” &c.

The Turks and Caicos Islands lie between 21° and 22° N. lat., and 71° and 72° 30' W. long.

The Government is administered by a President, who is subordinate to the Governor of Jamaica, and a Council composed of nominees and four elected members. All taxpayers who are able to read and write have the privilege of voting at elections. There are 250 registered electors, who return three members for the Turks Island and one for the Caicos Island. The election of members is regulated by Ordinances 1 and 3 of 1858.
