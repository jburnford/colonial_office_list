DOMINICA.

Dominica is situated between 15° 29' N. lat., and 61° 13' and 61° 30' W. long; is miles long and 16 broad; very mountainous; picturesque; was discovered by Christopher Columbus, on Sunday (hence its name) 3rd November, 1493, on his second voyage; lies between the French Islands of Guadeloupe and Martinique, and has an area of 280 square miles.

This island was included in the grant of sundry islands in the Caribbean Sea to Sir Robert Carlisle, by a patent, dated 2nd June, 1670; several attempts to bring the place under subjection to the English proved abortive. By treaty signed at Aix-la-Chapelle, in 1748, it was stipulated between the English and French that Dominica and some other islands should remain neutral, and the original proprietors, the Caribs, should be in possession. During the time that Dominica thus professedly regarded by the English as the French as a "neutral" island, many French planters settled on it and established plantations.

Dominica was captured by the English, and by the ninth article of the peace of 1763, was assigned to Great Britain. Commissioners were sent out for the purpose of surveying and selling lands capable of cultivation, which came to the British Crown the sum of £12,000 sterling. The French settlers were generally secured in their possessions, on condition of taking the oath of allegiance and paying a small rent. Dominica at this time formed one of the British Government, comprising also Grenada, the Grenadines, St. Vincent, and Tobago.

In 1771 the island was constituted a separate Government.

In 1771, the French, attracted by the beauty of the place, and invited over from Martinique some of their countrymen, prepared a naval and naval armament against the island, but the Marquis de Bouillé, Governor of Martinique, became masters of the island on the 7th September, after an obstinate resistance. Mr. Duchilleau, a cruel and tyrannical officer, was left as Governor; trade failed and great distress followed.

In 1783, the island was again restored to the English, and Sir John Ord, Bart., was appointed Governor.

In 1805, now the most remarkable event in the island for marking the time of its occupation and which goes by the name of La Gras (name of the invading General), the French landed at each flank of Roseau. The French troops and the militia fought gallantly, but fortunately Roseau, the capital, was set fire accidentally, and was obliged to capitulate to the enemy 12,000l. to quit; whilst the French, Sir George Prévost, and the troops (regular militia) proceeded across the island to the position of Prince Ruperts.

Serious riots occurred in 1893 in connection with the collection of taxes in the interior of the island. The police and marines were stoned, and ordered to fire on the rioters, with fatal results.

An inquiry into the administration was made by the Secretary of State, and entrusted to Sir Hamilton, formerly Governor of Tasmania, who left for Dominica in November, 1893, and returned to England in December of the same year.

It is estimated that about 150,000 acres of the island are at present uncultivated. Much
at one time under coffee, but "blight" and marauding of the maroons led to the abandonment of many estates, while the depreciation of land has still further reduced the area of cultivation. The soil and climate are adapted for the cultivation of coffee, cocoa, tea, nutmegs, spices, and tropical fruits of all kinds.

There are many thermal, chalybeate, sulphur, and other medicinal springs in the island, which is mic, and on the Grand Soufrière Hills there is a lesser or boiling lake at an elevation of 2,300 feet. An eruption in the great crater took place on January, 1880, ash and scoria covering the roofs of the houses in Roseau to a depth of two or three inches.

The south-east of the island are deposits of iron ore now being mined successfully.

Sugar, cocoa, and lime-juice are the chief products, but some fruit, coffee, spice, oils, and timber are also exported from the island. The principal exports in 1894 were: Coffee, 5,409 lbs., value £62,718; sugar, 1,220 hogsheads; lime-juice, value £10,964. The island abounds in rivers and rivulets, well stocked with fish. The average rainfall for 1894 was 87 inches.

The chief town is Roseau, population about 1,500; it is a port of registry, and had registered 1st December, 1894, five vessels, of a total tonnage of 54.

The Local Government is administered by an Administrator, aided by an Executive Council of members. In 1865 the Legislative Assembly was reduced, by an Act of the Local Legislature, from 9 nominated and 19 elected members to 7 nominated and 7 elected members. The Governor presides over the deliberations of the House and exercises a casting vote in the House, a deliberative as well as a casting vote in Committee.

Persons for a parish must possess land or houses assessed at £4 per annum, or be an occupier of land worth £1 per annum, or have an income of £5, or pay taxes of £5 per annum.

Two-thirds of the inhabitants speak a French patois.

MONTserrat.

This island, discovered by Columbus in 1493, named by him after a famous mountain where is situated the monastery in which Loyola conceived the project of founding Society of Jesus.
