
ST. HELENA.

Situation, Area, etc.

St. Helena is an island in the South Atlantic Ocean, 800 miles west of the Cape of Good Hope, from the coast of Africa, in 5° 32' S. lat., and 12° 56' W. long., about 100 miles long and 10 miles broad, covering an area of 47 square miles. It is 1000 miles from South America, 1000 miles from Africa, and 2000 miles from Europe. The usual mail passage is from England, 17 days.

The capital and only town is Jamestown, on the north-west side, with a population of about 2000. The climate is very healthy, the rainfall between 68" and 72" per annum. The soil is very fertile, the average death-rate for the three years ending March 31, 1900, was 10.00%, including sickness landed in the island during the year.

History.

St. Helena, then uninhabited and well wooded, was visited by Columbus in 1498, and by Jean de Nova Corrala, A.D. 1502. Edmund's Bay, May 24th, 1502. The Portuguese continued to keep
secret the situation of the island from other European nations until 1688, when it was visited by Captain Cavendish, on his return from a voyage round the world. They built a church there, but made no permanent settlement. The Dutch held it from 1645 to 1650, when they abandoned it. It was taken possession of by the East India Company in 1651, and a charter for its administration was granted in 1661. In 1665 it was seized by the Dutch, who were, however, expelled the same year. Again, in January, 1673, they seized it, to be driven out finally in May, by Captain Munden, of the English Navy. A new charter was issued by Charles II. to the East India Company for its possession in December, 1673, and it remained under that Company, with the exception of the period of Napoleon's imprisonment there, till 22nd April, 1824, when it was brought under the direct government of the Crown by an Act of Parliament of 1833.

Education and Local Government.

The Government maintain 4 schools, having 359 scholars. There are also 7 endowed and private day schools, one of which is aided by Government, having 387 scholars. School attendance is compulsory under Ordinance No. 4 of 1874, and fees of 1d. a week are charged.

The only local authority is the Poor Relief Board, the revenue and expenditure of which for 1897 were £636.1. and £504.

Industry.

St. Helena is well watered by clear springs, which are abundant. It is situated in the heart of the South Atlantic trade wind, blowing from the S.E. for about 330 days in the year, and in the direct track of vessels homeward bound from the East round the Cape of Good Hope. Previous to the opening of the overland route it was a port of call for a vast quantity of shipping and passengers to and from India and other parts of the East, and in consequence of its importance in connection with the Eastern trade, large establishments were maintained, both civil and military. For many years it was also a depot for liberated Africans landed from slavers captured by the West Coast squadron. As early as 1818, a law was passed that all future children born in the Island should be free.

Beyond the supply of the passing shipping it has never produced any article of export properly so called, except flax fibre. The company which started this industry failed from bad management in transporting the green leaves to town for treatment. For every 100 tons so transported at a heavy cost the yield was only about three tons of fibre. Hundreds of acres of flax are now going to waste. There is, however, said to be a field for capital in the preparation of the fibre from the *Furcraea gigantea*, a tree which grows wild all over the island. Its trade depends exclusively upon the intercourse and the visits of the Antarctic whalers and of ships in distress, and has greatly diminished in recent years in consequence of the opening of the telegraph cable.

The number of ships calling at Jamestown (now a free port) for supplies, including steamers and ships of war, was in 1885, 450; in 1886, 377; in 1887, 317; in 1888, 330; in 1889, 288; in 1890, 211; in 1891, 211; in 1892, 223; in 1893, 199; in 1894, 195; in 1895, 203; 1896, 151; 1897, 147; 1898, 147. It is a port of registry.

The chief industries are fishing and agriculture, the main arable crop being potatoes.

There are no private banks in the Colony, but a Government savings bank was established in 1865 (total deposits on 31st December, 1898—£17,008), and the Government issues bills of exchange on England at from ¼ to 1 per cent.

Means of Communication.

Steamers arrive from England at regular intervals of 4 weeks, and proceed to the Cape and Natal; steamers arrive from thence at the same interval, and proceed to England. The interval between the arrival and departure of the mails is 10 days. The rate of postage to the U.K., India, and certain Colonies* is 1d. per ½ oz.; to other parts of the world 2½d. per ½ oz. There is no internal post, but there are 3½ miles of telegraph, constructed by the Colonial Government and the War Office at a cost of 700l. The revenues in 1898 were 93l., and the expenses 211l. The net postal revenue in 1898 was 259l., and the expenditure 349l. The new cable from the Cape reached St. Helena at the end of 1899, and was carried forward to Ascension by February, 1900. It is now being completed to St. Vincent.

Defence.

Jamestown has been made a second-class imperial coaling station, and extensive defensive works have been constructed on the heights overlooking the harbour.

Constitution.

The Government is administered by a Governor, aided by an Executive Council.

The Governor alone makes Ordinances, there being no Legislative Council, but power is reserved to legislate by Order of Her Majesty in Council.
