The length of open line is 429 miles, as follows:—Province Wellesley, Pétrak, Selangor, Negri Sembilan, and Malacca, 23, 190, 113, 82, and 21 miles respectively. There are also 21 miles in the Negri Sembilan worked by a company. The length of line under construction is 120 miles in the state of Johore. The total length of line surveyed is 200 miles, 181 miles in Pahang and 19 miles in Pétrak. The length under survey is 139 miles in Pahang. Through communication between Prai in Province Wellesley to Gemas in the Negri Sembilan, a distance of 352 miles, was established on 1st October, 1906.



The total length of line from Prai to Singapore, when completed, excluding branches, will be about 500 miles. The branch lines run to Port Weld, Teluk Anson, Batu Caves, Port Swettenham and Port Dickson.



There is also a steam ferry service between Penang and Prai, the northern terminus of the system in Province Wellesley, a distance of 5 miles.

Pétrak.



H.H. Sir Idris Mersid-el-Aizam Sham, G.C.M.G., Sultan.



Pétrak is the most northerly of the Protected States on the west coast, touching Province Wellesley on the north and Selangor on the south. It is one of the oldest States in the Peninsula, and, though subject to Achin at one time, and overrun by Kedah in 1821, it generally maintained its independence. About 1850 the discovery of tin in Larut was followed by the immigration of Chinese in great numbers. Since the appointment of a British Resident in 1874, the record of the State has been one of remarkable progress, interrupted only by the murder of the first British Resident, Mr. J. W. Birch, in 1875, the military occupation of the country, and the banishment to the Seychelles of the ex-Sultan and some chiefs implicated in the affair.



The State, which has an area of 6,550 square miles, is well watered, the Pétrak, with its tributaries the Kinta, Batang Padang, Bidor and Sungkai, being the most important rivers. Other rivers of importance are the Krian, the Kurau and the Bernam, with its tributary, the Slim. The mountains on the east boundary, which are part of the main range of the Peninsula, rise to a height of over 7,000 feet.



The chief export is tin. Sugar and rice are extensively grown. The cultivation of rubber is now attracting great attention, and such consignments as have been exported have realised very high prices. In the Krian district the Government has completed an extensive scheme for the irrigation of more than 51,000 acres of rice land, the larger proportion of which is already under cultivation.



Larut, Kinta and Batang Padang are the chief mining districts. The district of Batang Padang lies between the Kinta valley and the Selangor boundary, and contains rich deposits of tin, a little gold and wolfram.



The total mileage of completed roads in Pétrak is 661 miles of metalled cart roads, and 627 miles of bridle and other paths.



The whole length of the State is traversed by the main line of railway from Penang.



The capital is Taiping, in the Larut district, where the British Resident resides.



The chief towns are Ipoh, Kuala Kangsar, Kampar, Tapah and Batu Gajah, and the two ports are Teluk Anson and Port Weld. His Highness the Sultan of Pétrak resides at Kuala Kangsar.



Selangor.



H.H. Sulaiman bin Almerhum Raja Musa, C.M.G., Sultan.



Selangor situated on the Western side of the Peninsula, is bounded on the North by Pétrak, on the East and South-east by Pahang and Negri Sembilan, and on the West by the Straits of Malacca.



The total area is estimated at 3,200 square miles, with a coast line extending for about 125 miles. Where the State touches the central range of the Peninsula there are several peaks of over 5,000 feet and one of 5,812 feet.



The State is drained by four rivers: The Bernam, forming the boundary between Pétrak and Selangor, the Selangor, the Klang and the Langat. The inhabitants are said to be the descendants of a Colony from the Celebes.

The climate is uniform, and may be described as hot and moist. The nights are cool. The average maximum temperature in the low country is 91 and the average minimum temperature is 71. The rainfall varies considerably, as much as 124 inches having been registered. The wettest seasons are during March and April, and from October to December.



For administrative purposes the State is divided into six districts—viz., Kuala Lumpur, Klang, Ulu Langat, Ulu Selangor, Kuala Langat and Kuala Selangor.



The capital of the State is Kuala Lumpur, which is also the headquarters of the administration of the Federated Malay States. It is the largest town on the mainland of the Peninsula, with a population of 80,000. It has a water supply from an impounding reservoir 7 miles distant, and is also lighted by electricity. There are 5½ miles of town streets. It also possesses a picturesque public garden, covering about 180 acres, and several imposing Government buildings, the principal being the Government offices of modern Saracenic design, with a clock tower 130 feet high, town hall, museum, courts, prison, railway station and offices, and a new post office now under construction. There is also a branch of the Chartered Bank of India, Australia and China. The currency is the Straits Settlements dollar, with a fixed exchange at two shillings and fourpence. The telegraph system extends all over the State, and the telephone, with the exchange at Kuala Lumpur, is worked over about 400 miles of wire.



There are 53 schools in the State; 46 are maintained by Government for education in native languages, the remainder are grants-in-aid schools.



Port Swettenham, situated at the mouth of the Klang River and 27 miles by rail from Kuala Lumpur, is the chief port, and has wharves capable of accommodating ocean-going steamers. The aggregate tonnage of vessels registered during 1905 was 895,935.



The total revenue of the State during 1905 amounted to $8,857,793, and the expenditure to $7,186,146.



There is no public debt.



The principal sources of revenue are customs, excise, railways and land. The only import duties are those charged on opium and spirituous liquors. In 1905 the gross total value of imports was $24,643,619, and the exports $26,633,315.



The chief industry of the State is tin mining. The amount of tin exported in 1905 was 289,686 piculs, valued at $23,412,558. The area alienated for mining is 65,573 acres, and there are about 74,000 Chinese employed exclusively in this industry. Coffeplanting under European supervision made good progress for some years, but received a severe check owing to the fall in price. There are about 18,000 acres under cocoanuts. Rice, gambier, pepper and other products are grown with success. Of recent years the cultivation of rubber has made great strides, especially in the coast districts, which appear to be the locality most in favour by rubber prospectors. Most of the large estates have been converted into and sold to limited liability companies. At the end of 1905 the area alienated for rubber cultivation was about 26,000 acres, of which about 20,000 acres were planted up. The value of rubber exported during 1905 was estimated at $368,801.



There are 476 miles of metalled and 64 miles of unmetalled cart roads, and 215 miles of bridle paths.



Negri Sembilan (Nine States.)



H.H. Tungku Mohamed, C.M.G., Yang di Pertuan of Sri Menanti and the Chiefs.



The Federal State of the Negri Sembilan, as at present constituted, dates from 1895, but a Federation under that title already existed in the last century. The present federation consists of the following States, viz., Sungei Ujong, Jelebu, Johol, and Rembau, and of the six smaller States of Ulu Muar, Jempol, Terachi, Gunong Pasir, Inas, Tampin, and Gemencheh. The headquarters of the Resident are at Seremban; and there are district officers at Port Dickson, on the coast, Kwala Klawang in Jelebu, Kwala Pilah in Ulu Muar, and Tampin. The total mileage of roads in the Negri Sembilan is 388 miles of cart roads, of which 255 miles are metalled, and 133 miles of bridle paths.



The revenue is derived mainly from tin and agriculture. The miners are Chinese, the Malay population being almost entirely agricultural. Gold reefs exist in Gemencheh and on the Muar River. Coffee has been successfully cultivated, and there are many large tapioca and gambier estates. Rubber has also been extensively planted in recent years. There is also a considerable production of rice. A great part of the State is mountainous, the rest consisting of undulating country. The whole State is well watered, the chief rivers being the Muar and the Linggi, the latter of which forms, for some distance, the boundary between the State and the territory of Malacca.



Pahang.



H.H. Sir Ahmad Mattham Shah Almerhum Ali, K.C.M.G., Sultan.



Pahang, the largest of the Protected Native States, is on the east coast of the Peninsula. Its native rulers have for many years looked southward, of later years to the Colony, for protection against their northern neighbours.



Gunong Tahan, the highest point in the State (7,050 feet), was ascended for the first time in 1905 by Mr. H. C. Robinson. It lies on or near the northern boundary between Pahang and Kelantan.



The State is drained by one of the largest rivers in the Peninsula, if not the largest, the Pahang River, which is fed by the Lipis, Jelai, Tembeling, Semantan, Triang, Bera Luit, Lepar and other rivers. To the north the Kuantan and to the south the Rompin and Endau also debouch into the China Sea, the last named forming the boundary between Pahang and Johore.



These rivers are unfortunately so shallow as to be navigable for only steam launches and other small craft, except in the rainy season, and during the north-east monsoon their mouths are difficult of access.



The country is rich in gold, tin and other minerals, and several companies, under European or Australian direction, are at work. The capital has hitherto been Pekan, near the mouth of the Pahang River, where the Sultan still resides, but the seat of government has been removed 200 miles up stream to Kuala Lipis. A line of telegraph has been carried from Selangor over the dividing range to Lipis in this State, and a cart road, 83 miles in length, has been constructed from Kuala

Kubu, on the F.M.S. Railway, to Kuala Lipis. There is a daily motor car service on this road. A branch road has also been made with Government assistance from Tras to Bentong, a distance of some 20 miles, for the purpose of opening up the mines in that district, and trunk roads from Bentong to Kuala Pilah in the Negri Sembilan, and from Bentong to Kuala Lumpor by the Sempak Pass, are under construction.



In the Kuantan district a cart road is being made from the Blat River to the Lepar watershed to open up the tin fields of that part of the State.
