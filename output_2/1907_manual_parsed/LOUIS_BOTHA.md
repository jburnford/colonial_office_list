Prior to this, the Civil Administration was partly organised on a provisional basis, resident magistrates and district Commissioners having been appointed in many districts. Numerous proclamations were issued to provide for administrative requirements, and amongst those issued in 1902 may be mentioned those making provision for the acquisition of lands for public purposes (Proclamation 7), placing subsidised schools under the control of the Director of Education (9), amending the law relating to the registration of deeds (10), levying a tax of 10 per cent. on the annual net produce of gold-bearing produce (34), regulating the registration of mining rights (35, amended by Ordinance 6 of 1902), and several relating to the administration of justice. On the 21st of June, 1902, Letters Patent were published constituting the Government of the Transvaal, and providing for an Executive and a Legislative Council, the members of which are to be appointed by the Crown; the subsequent legislation being accordingly by Ordinance.

On the 31st March, 1905, Letters Patent were passed providing for the constitution of a Legislative Assembly to consist of the Lieut.-Governor and not less than six or more than nine official members, and (provisionally) not less than thirty or more than thirty-five elected members. But in December, 1906, this Constitution was revoked and a new one substituted.

Constitution.

On December 6th, 1906, Letters Patent were issued conferring full responsible government upon the Transvaal. The new legislature consists of two chambers. The Legislative Council contains 15 members, who will in the first instance be nominated by the Governor for a period of five years. Appointments to fill any vacancies which may occur will be made by the Governor in Council, by whom also the President will be appointed. But it will be lawful at any time after four years from the date of the first meeting of the Council for the Legislature to pass a law providing for the election of members of the Legislative Council. Members of the Council must be of the age of 30 years or upwards, must have resided in the Colony for three years, and must possess the electoral qualification. The Legislative Assembly contains 69 members, of whom 34 represent the Witwatersrand area. Members are returned throughout the Transvaal by single-member constituencies, but the old magisterial districts are retained as separate units, and sub-divided where a district is entitled to more than one member, the divisions following as far as possible the boundaries of the old field countries or magisterial wards. Every white male British subject is entitled to be registered as a voter if he has resided in the Transvaal for six months, either immediately before the framing of the register or within three years, in case of temporary absence during those six months. The qualification for membership of the Assembly is the same as the voter's qualification. There will be an automatic re-distribution of seats, on a voters' basis, at the end of every four years. The Legislative Assembly and any elected Legislative Council will be dissolved at the end of five years, or earlier at the option of the Governor. The Speaker of the Assembly will be elected by the members, and will on election vacate his seat. The language of debate in the Legislature will be either English or Dutch, the language of record being English, while copies of votes and proceedings, and of all proposed laws, will be printed in both languages, in accordance with the practice observed in Cape Colony. It is provided that, in the event of disagreement between the Legislative Council and the Legislative Assembly, the Governor may convene a joint sitting of the members of both Houses, presided over by the Speaker of the Assembly, when the matter at issue shall be decided by an absolute majority of
the members of both Houses. Members of the Legislature will receive as payment for their services a fixed sum of £150 and £2 a day for each day of attendance up to a maximum of £300 in any one year. The Legislature will have power to alter or repeal any of the provisions of the Letters Patent, but any bill effecting such repeal or alteration must be reserved.

A special clause in the Letters Patent provides that whereas it is His Majesty's will and pleasure that all persons within his dominions shall be free from any conditions of employment or residence of a servile character, the Governor shall reserve any law providing for the introduction into the Colony under contract, indenture or licence of labourers from places outside South Africa.

Another clause provides for the termination of the system of labour deriving effect from "The Labour Importation Ordinance, 1904," and all Ordinances amending the same, at the end of one year from the date of the first meeting of the Legislature. Under this clause no licence can be issued for the introduction of labourers into the Colony under the Ordinance, and no contract can be renewed under its authority after the commencement of the Letters Patent. The Legislature of the Colony may accelerate the determination of the said system of labour or regulate it subject to the conditions of the Letters Patent, and may provide for all necessary matters and for carrying into effect the declarations and enactments of the Letters Patent in that behalf.

Provision is made in regard to the interests of the natives by enacting that no lands which have been or may hereafter be set aside for their occupation shall be alienated or diverted from this purpose, except by legislation which will be liable to be reserved for the signification of His Majesty's pleasure. The Governor will continue to exercise over all Chiefs and natives in the Colony the authority vested in him as paramount Chief, and the Governor in Council is empowered to convene an assembly of native Chiefs to enable the natives to make known their views on questions affecting their interests.

Special provision is made in the Letters Patent with regard to the Land Settlement scheme which was established during the period of Crown Colony government. The Governor is to appoint and control for five years a Land Board consisting of three members, which will be a body corporate, and will exercise the rights and duties conferred and imposed upon the Government of the Transvaal in respect of the existing settlers by any Law or Agreement.

To this Land Board will be transferred all lands and movable property held by existing settlers, and any sums approved by a Secretary of State out of moneys hereafter appropriated by the Inter-Colonial Council for Land Settlement purposes. The Government of the Colony may (subject to the consent of the Governor, and with the approval of the Secretary of State) come to an agreement with the Board for determining the functions of the Board at an earlier date.

The arrangement under which the Inter-Colonial Council has administered certain services common to the Transvaal and Orange River Colony will be liable to be terminated on the motion of either Colony. Either Government may give notice to the other after Responsible Government has been established in both Colonies, and the Inter-Colonial Council will cease to exist, or the particular service to which the notice relates will be terminated, after six months from the date of such notice. The apportionment of the assets and liabilities of the Council, unless an agreement is arrived at between the two Governments, will be referred to arbitration, or to the Privy Council if both Governments do not agree to arbitration.

The Election for the first Legislative Assembly under the new constitution will take place early in 1907.

Customs Union.

On 23rd March, 1903, a Customs Agreement was signed, under which the Transvaal and Southern Rhodesia entered the Customs Union, the result being that a common tariff was established for all the South African Colonies. All transit dues were abolished, and considerable reductions were made in through railway rates. The list of South African products which are carried over the railways at preferential rates—this constituting a second line of Protection—was reduced from 110 to 58 articles. A rebate is granted on articles manufactured in the United Kingdom of 25% of the duty in certain cases and of the whole duty in those cases where it is 2½% ad valorem (ironmongery, etc.); a similar rebate may be made to any British colony giving equivalent reciprocal privileges to the Union. This Union was superseded by a new Agreement in 1906, which provided for a general increase in the Customs duties. The Agreement maintained the principle of preference for imports from the United Kingdom and reciprocating Colonies, a rebate equal to the increase in the duties being granted on such imports.

Local Government.

Municipal Government was established throughout the Colony by Ordinance enacted in 1903, as extended and amended in 1904, 1905 and 1906. These Ordinances give Local Authorities very extensive powers—interference by the Government being practically limited to powers of audit, the limitation of local loans, the approval of schemes for municipal works and the alienation of municipal property. The basis of municipal qualification is the ownership of real property to the value of £100, or the tenancy of premises of the value of £300, or annual value of £24, but the franchise is not extended to aliens or to coloured British subjects. Thirty-five municipalities have already been established and considerable progress in the improvement of the towns has been made.

The integral unity of the Witwatersrand area has been recognised by the establishment under Ordinance No. 48 of 1904 of the Rand Water Board composed of representatives of the six Municipal Councils in the area and of the Chamber of Mines, which is charged with the task of obtaining water for and supplying it in bulk to its constituent authorities; and further by the creation under Ordinance No. 9 of 1906 of the Rand Provisional Joint Committee, composed of representatives of the Municipal Councils within the area, with the duty of dealing with Hospital matters, the prevention of epidemic diseases in the area and the upkeep of the Main Reef Road—a main trunk road traversing practically the whole of the area.

Local Self-Government in rural districts has not yet been inaugurated, though in hospital matters provision is made for the formation of Joint Committees representative of Urban and Rural Committees, and further legislation was enacted in 1905 and 1906 providing for the creation of Health Committees where the density of the population makes it necessary for sanitary measures to be enforced.

In June, 1904, a Commission was appointed to inquire into the financial relations between the Central Government and the various Municipal Governments. This Commission has already issued three reports and will shortly issue its fourth and final report, completing an exhaustive survey of the important subjects which were referred to its consideration. Many of the recommendations of this Commission have already been adopted and the remainder will be dealt with after the institution of Responsible Government in the Colony.

Land.

The Land Department was constituted by the Crown Land Disposal Ordinance passed in 1902, and was preceded by a Land Board appointed by the High Commissioner in December, 1901. The Department is charged with the settlement of Crown Lands for agricultural purposes on terms of conditional purchase in connection with which the Settlers' Ordinance provides for cash advances to settlers.

The functions of the Department include the disposal by sale or otherwise of Government Erven and the reservation of land for public and other purposes; all matters which affect the administration of Crown Lands are dealt with by the Department.

The Occupation Farms Ordinance, 1904, is administered by the Department, as are also several minor Ordinances affecting town lands, etc.

Education.

The enrolment in the 417 Government elementary schools upon the 30th June, 1906, was 30,641. The grand total of scholars receiving education in Government schools, including high schools in Pretoria, Johannesburg and Potchefstroom, and secondary schools in the smaller towns of the Colony, was 32,360. The University College in Johannesburg provided education of university standard (chiefly in subjects connected with mining and engineering) to over 50 students.

During Mr. Chamberlain's stay in Johannesburg, an arrangement was made between him and a large representative body of the colonists that a loan of £5,000,000 should be issued, guaranteed
by the Imperial Government, and secured on the general revenue of the Transvaal and Orange River Colonies, bearing interest at 3% per annum, with a sinking fund of 1%. It was understood that the proceeds of this loan would be used for the extinction of liabilities, the purchase of public works and railways, and other local requirements. It was further understood that a further loan of £30,000,000, bearing interest at 4%, should be issued, and that the proceeds of this loan should be paid to the Imperial Government as a contribution towards the cost of the war. This issue was to take place in three instalments of £10,000,000 each, at an interval of at least twelve months between each, and the first instalment was to be raised about January, 1904, the success of this issue being guaranteed by certain South African firms. The loan was to be a charge on the general revenue of the Transvaal.

The necessary legislation having been passed in the Imperial Parliament and the local Legislature, the first of these loans, to the extent of £30,000,000, was issued (at par) in May, 1903. Throughout this year, however, there was considerable depression in the Colony, and, as this state of things continued, His Majesty's Government have not pressed for the raising of the second or war loan.

The balance of the £35,000,000 loan (£5,000,000) was raised about the middle of 1904. This loan was put up to tender, and realised an average price of 98½. 10s. 3d. By that time the whole of the first issue had been practically expended, counting in the amounts ear-marked for definite impending liabilities—such as the redemption of the shares of the Netherlands Railway Company. The heavy task of repairing the ravages of war and relieving the necessities of all classes of people in the new Colonies was dealt with from four funds, viz.:

1. The £3,000,000 mentioned in the Terms of Surrender, and paid as a free grant from Imperial funds.

2. £5,000,000 appropriated for "repatriation and compensation" from the guaranteed loan. Both of these funds were fully spent by the middle of 1904, and a debit of about £600,000 incurred; the expenditure had, however, almost ceased, and it is anticipated that the debit will eventually be extinguished by the repayment for supplies made in excess of the £3,000,000.

3. £2,000,000 allotted to compensation to "British subjects, foreign subjects, and natives." This, like (1), was a free grant from Imperial funds. More than half of it was paid by the 30th April, 1904, to persons who had suffered losses from the war, at the rate of 13s. 4d. in the pound on their assessed losses, and the final instalment of 4s. in the pound brought the rate up to 17s. 4d., a very remarkable degree of compensation. £300,000 was set aside for the compensation of natives.

4. £2,000,000 paid by the War Office for disbursement to the "protected burghers," being men to whom the military authorities had incurred some special obligation.

To this was added a further sum of £2,500,000 to discharge the military receipts given during the war. This must be distinguished from the previous funds, as it was required for the payment of debts which formed part of the expenses of the war; the payments, however, in common with those under the previous heads, assisted the people to re-build, re-stock, and make a fresh start in their occupations. The amount actually paid has slightly exceeded the £2,500,000.

Thus the total amount provided for relief was £14,500,000, of which £2,500,000 was in payment for goods received.

When the guaranteed loan was about to be issued, several difficulties arose as to the allocation of the proceeds, as there were various matters in which both of the new Colonies were interested, and it was accordingly decided to treat the railways as the joint property of the two Colonies, and to establish a common fund for that and the like common services. For these purposes the Inter-Colonial Council was constituted. (See under "South Africa.") Almost the whole production is from reef, the alluvial gold being inconsiderable in amount. In 1897, the average number of persons employed by gold mines was 9,530 whites and 69,127 natives. These numbers rose to 12,000 and 107,000 just before the war in 1899, but then fell to about 10,000 and 45,000 in December, 1902. In August, 1905, the whites numbered 17,429 and the natives 100,081. The wages in 1904 were from 20s. a day for skilled white workmen, and 52s. monthly for natives; the latter are also provided with housing and food, at a cost of about 10s. per month each. Chinese labourers were imported under an ordinance passed in February, 1904, and at the end of 1905 over 47,000 were on the Rand; the scheme was then stopped by H.M.'s Government, pending a decision as to the grant of responsible government to the colony; but outstanding licences for the importation of 14,700 Chinese could not be interfered with.

The main reef on the Witwatersrand, continuously traced, measures about 62 miles, and between 1884 and the end of June, 1906, produced about 156,380,181l. of gold. Since the British occupation great activity has been displayed in boring operations, especially in the far eastern and western extensions of the Rand; but in the outside districts prospecting is for promising quartz and for other surface indications. Considerable prospecting for diamonds is being carried on in the Pretoria district. The Premier (Transvaal) Diamond Mining Company is now one of the largest diamond producers of the world; its production up to the end of June, 1905, being 1,456,345 carats.
