ADEN COLONY

Area

Aden Colony is situated about 100 miles east of the Straits of Bab el Mandeb on the south coast of Arabia. The area is 75 square miles.

Geographical Features

The Colony consists of two extinct volcanic craters forming rocky peninsulas, joined by a flat sandy foreshore, comprising:

(a) The peninsula on which are situated the main town known as Crater, the wharf and shipyard area of Maala, the modern harbour suburb known as Tawahi, adjacent to which is an area leased to the Air Ministry for R.A.F. and military purposes and known collectively as Steamer Point.

(b) The isthmus known as Khormaksar where a large R.A.F. station is situated.

(c) The strip of mainland between the two peninsulas which encloses the modern harbour and contains the township of Sheikh Othman and the villages of Hiswa and Imad.

(d) The Little Aden peninsula where a modern refinery has been constructed by the Aden Petroleum Refinery Limited and where the two fishing villages of Bureika and Fukum lie.

Climate

The climate is not unhealthy, though the heat and humidity are trying between April and October. During the north-east monsoon, that is, between the latter part of October and April, the climate is generally cool and pleasant. The rainfall is extremely scanty and in some years non-existent, but as much as eight inches have been known to fall in a year.

History

The plundering of an Indian ship on the coast near Aden led to the capture of Aden by a successful expedition sent from Bombay by the East India Company in 1839 under Major Bailey and Captain Haines of the Indian Navy.

The need to establish coaling stations on trade routes because of the replacement of sailing ships by steamers was one of the reasons which led to the occupation of Aden by the British. Little Aden was obtained by purchase in 1868. The coastal strip between the two peninsulas was secured by purchase in 1882 and 1888.

Under the provisions of the Government of India Act, 1935, and the Aden Colony Order, 1936, the Chief Commissioner’s Province of Aden ceased to be a part of British India and was constituted as a Colony of Aden from 1st April 1937.

Constitution

By Additional Royal Instructions issued in November 1958, the following changes in the composition of the Executive Council of the Colony have become effective:

The Executive Council now consists of five ex officio members (who must include the Chief Secretary, the Attorney-General, the Financial Secretary, and a representative of the Senior Officer of the Forces stationed in the Colony), and also “five persons who are Elected Members or
Nominated Members of the Legislative Council, but not less than three of whom shall be Elected Members, selected and appointed by the Governor who are in consequence of the Aden Colony Royal Instructions, 1961, styled Ministers. There is provision for the Governor to assign to individual Ministers responsibility for matters which he may prescribe at his discretion. Under the provisions of the Aden Colony (Amendment) Order, 1944, a Legislative Council was constituted consisting of the Governor as President, four ex officio members, and not more than eight unofficial members. The Aden Colony (Amendment) Order, 1955, provided for the introduction of an elected element into the Legislative Council. Under this Order the Council consisted of the Governor as President, four ex officio members, not more than five nominated official members, not more than five nominated unofficial members, and four elected members.

The Aden Colony (Amendment) Order, 1958, provided for a Legislative Council consisting of a Speaker, five ex officio members, twelve elected members, and six nominated members. The first elections under the provisions of this Order took place on 4th January 1959.

**Land Policy**

Under the Crown Lands Ordinance, 1955, the Governor may grant lease or alienate on Her Majesty's behalf any Crown Land for building, agricultural or any other purpose, on any terms and conditions, as he may think fit.

The administration of the Crown Lands Ordinance is under the charge of a Commissioner of Lands who is appointed by the Governor.

**Taxation**

Aden is a free port and there is no general Customs tariff, but taxes are levied on alcoholic and other beverages, tobacco and manufactures thereof, non-potable alcoholic mixtures, qat, aerated waters and petroleum products. The Income Tax Ordinance, 1961, provides for the payment of a graduated tax by individuals ranging from Sh. 1/- to Sh. 15/- in the £ on chargeable income. Companies pay at the rate of Sh.6/50 in the £.

**Currency**

The currency in circulation is the East African currency issued by the East African Currency Board in denominations of 100 Sh., 20 Sh., 10 Sh., and 5 Sh. notes and 1 Sh., 50 cents, 10 cents, 5 cents and 1 cent coins.

**Education**

The Educational system consists of 4 years primary, 3 years of intermediate and 4–6 years of secondary schooling. Primary and Intermediate education is provided free for Adenese boys and girls; moderate fees are charged for secondary education (these may be waived for poor pupils), while allowances are paid to teachers in training. In addition, generous grants are made to Grant-in-Aid Schools. There are 18 Government Primary Schools, 13 Grant-Aided and 6 Private Schools providing education for 6,098 boys and 3,150 girls. Intermediate education is provided at 6 Government, 11 Grant-Aided and 4 Private Schools for 3,307 boys and 1,240 girls. Secondary Education is provided mainly at the
3 Government schools (2 for boys and 1 for girls) and the Technical Institute in Maala with its branch at Little Aden, providing a total of 1,753 places. A new Men's Teacher Training Centre has been opened and provides a 2 year course (83 men were enrolled in 1960). A modern women's Teacher Training Centre (which had 20 students in 1960) is nearing completion. Adult Education is provided by part-time and evening classes at the Technical Institute, by evening G.C.E. classes, special classes for the Police, Arabic Language classes for non-Arabs, and various courses for women at the Besse Centre. An agreed proportion of Protectorate students enter Colony Education Institutions.

The Education Department also undertakes a considerable amount of administration for the Overseas Studies Board and in January 1960 an Aden Students' Office was established in London with a Students' Liaison Officer. At the end of 1960 there were 77 scholars taking courses in higher education abroad; of these, 66 were provided for by the Government, including 9 Departmental Trainees. In addition, there were 41 private sponsored students as well as a considerable number of private students.

The expenditure on Colony education has risen from £6,600 in 1936 to £524,814 in 1960-61. There is provision under the current Development Plan (1960-64) for capital expenditure estimated at £550,000.

Libraries

The Lake Library, administered by the Aden Municipality, contains 20,654 books in English, Arabic and Urdu and has 1,148 members. A Travelling Library with 5,858 volumes in English and Arabic has 492 members. There are well-stocked and expanding libraries at Aden College, the Technical Institute, the Boys' Secondary School and the Girls' College. Amenities for the teaching profession include a club with a library of over 2,000 volumes.

Communications

The Civil Air Terminal is located at Khormaksar some 7 miles from the seaport and the aerodrome lies across the isthmus between the upper reaches of the inner harbour and the shore of the gulf of Aden. The aerodrome is jointly used by the Royal Air Force and the Department of Civil Aviation. The main runway which is 10 feet above sea-level is bitumenised and has a length of 8,386 feet, and is of a construction to permit its use by the heaviest of modern aircraft. Technical services for the common use of military and civil aircraft are jointly provided by the Royal Air Force and the Department of Civil Aviation.

Ten airlines operate scheduled services from, to and through the Civil Air Terminal: Aden Airways, Air-India International, Alitalia, B.O.A.C., East African Airways Corporation, Ethiopian Air Lines, Middle East Airlines, Saudi Arabian Airlines, Sudan Airways and United Arab Airlines. Three of these airlines operate Comet or Boeing 707 jet aircraft.

Operating a fleet of 7 DC3 and 3 Argonaut aircraft, the local airline, Aden Airways, Limited, an associate of B.O.A.C., maintains a domestic network of regular services to destinations in the Protectorate as well as scheduled services on international trunk routes to East Africa, Ethiopia, Somalia, Saudi Arabia, Oman, Bahrain and Egypt.

The Colony has a total of 112·2 miles of roads, of which 60·9 are asphalted. There are no railways.

The vessels of the principal steamship companies call regularly at Aden and the number of vessels which entered the Port of Aden during 1960, exclusive of country craft, was 5,972, with a tonnage of 27,646,403. The number of country craft which entered the Port during the same period was 1,495 with an aggregate tonnage of 136,522. There are 22 first-class berths and 6 second-class berths. The first-class berths are suitable for ships of from 350 to 800 feet in length. The second-class berths are suitable for ships of about 300 feet in length. Thirteen of the first-class berths are fitted with submarine pipelines for fuelling ships. There is also an 800 feet long quay with a depth alongside of 18 feet suitable for coasting vessels of up to 2,500 tons gross. Transit and storage sheds on the public wharves provide a total covered space of 400,000 square feet. The approach channel is dredged to 36 feet, and three of the largest fuelling berths are dredged to 39 feet. The Oil Harbour at Little Aden has four jetty berths, the approach channel being dredged to 38 feet and the Oil Harbour to 40 feet.

Broadcasting

The Aden Broadcasting Service, a part of the Public Relations and Information Department, inaugurated in 1954 transmits for 11 hours daily in Arabic on short wave 41·8 metres and medium wave 375 metres. The transmitters now in use are 7kW. and 5kW. respectively, and are situated at Hiswa. It is estimated that about 30,000 receivers are in use in the Colony and a similar number in the Protectorate.
Principal Occupations

Of the estimated local labour force of 62,223, some 7,490 are engaged in Port activities such as bunkering, handling of cargo, ship repairing, manning harbour vessels, and so forth; 9,026 in building and construction, 1,800 in oil refining, 9,140 in industrial undertakings, 7,137 in retail and wholesale trades, 6,884 in Government and Services Departments, 746 in miscellaneous trades, and 20,000 in the hotel and catering trade and in private domestic service.

Main Products

There is no agriculture in the Colony and the only important local product is salt which is made from sea water by solar evaporation; production in 1960 was 127,641 long tons.

The amount of fish caught in Colony waters tends to be somewhat erratic, but in a good season the total landings may be in the region of 5,000 tons, of which over 1,000 tons is consumed locally, the surplus being sold overseas through exporters of salt and dried fish. The overall catch rate has been improved through the greater use by local fishermen of nylon fishing nets and mechanized boats based on the local pattern. The bulk of landings in the Colony consists of King-fish, and Indian Mackerel, Tunny and various types of Horse Mackerel. The Aden Government Fisheries vessel "Gulf Explorer" is extensively used on active fishing operations in order to consolidate developments already achieved and to extend overall development policy of the Fisheries Department.

The Oil Refinery at Little Aden produces refined petroleum from imported crude oil. In July 1961 the Refinery had been in commission for seven years and in that time had processed over 25 million tons of crude oil. The products were exported either by tanker or as fuel for ships' bunkers; a small percentage was retained in Aden to meet local demand.

Because of its favourable geographical position and its efficient service to ships, Aden has become one of the busiest oil bunkering ports in the world.

There are small factories in the Colony which produce a variety of items: soap, carbon dioxide, recombined acetylene milk, soft drinks, aluminium pots and pans, cigarettes, dyed and printed cloth, wooden furniture, bricks, tiles and retreaded tyres. There is a small but flourishing industry for the blending and packing of perfumes, hair oil and toilet waters; a modern mechanical mill crushes oil seeds. Some of the Colony produce is sold locally and some is exported to neighbouring territories.

The trade of Aden is mainly transhipment and entrepôt, the port serving as a centre of distribution to and from neighbouring territories. Traditional trade is mainly in textiles, gums and resins, grains and flour, coffee, tea, hides and skins, raw cotton and cottonseed and cheap consumer goods. There is also trade in dates, sugar, spices, oils, tobacco and shells and a flourishing trade in luxury goods such as cameras, watches, binoculars and cosmetics, which are sold to passengers coming ashore from ships refuelling in Aden Harbour.

Marketing and Co-operative Organizations

The Central Wholesale Produce Market, opened at Khormaksar in 1959, is administered by the Adviser on Co-operative Development as the Authority under the provisions of the Produce Marketing Ordinance, 1958. Its purpose is to rationalize the wholesale of fruit and vegetables in the interests of both the producers in the Protectorate and the consumers in the Colony.

The Co-operative and Marketing Department has introduced co-operation to the fishermen for fishing requisites in the Colony waters and two societies have been formed. One co-operative canteen amongst a section of civil servants has been successfully launched and two others are in process of formation.

Co-operative Societies Rules, 1961, under the Co-operative Societies Ordinance, 1957, were made by the Governor-in-Council in August 1961.
